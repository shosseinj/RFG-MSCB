\textbf{Boundary-Frequency Injection Module:}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Decomposing frequency components~

  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    Frequency Feature Decomposer (FFD)
  \item
    Spilt skip features into high-frequency and low-frequency using 2D
    FFT

    \begin{itemize}
    \item
      High-frequency -\textgreater{} Boundaries, edges
    \item
      Low-frequency -\textgreater{} semantic content
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Using boundary information for better feature guidance

  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    Boundary-guided Squeeze-Excitation Injection (BSEI)
  \item
    Uses Sobel operators to detect boundaries in skip features
  \item
    Creates spatial attention maps from boundary information
  \item
    Uses cross-attention between decoder features and boundary-weighted
    encoder features
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Adaptively fusing these different feature representations

  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    Multi-scale Frequency Adaptive Fusion (MFAF)
  \item
    Learns soft weights to combine high-frequency, low-frequency, and
    boundary-enhanced features
  \item
    Uses channel-wise gating to decide the importance of each component
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Purpose:}

The BFIM replaces standard skip connections to:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Better preserve boundary information}~during up-sampling
\item
  \textbf{Adaptively emphasize}~important frequency components
\item
  \textbf{Improve segmentation accuracy}~especially for object
  boundaries
\item
  \textbf{Provide learnable fusion}~instead of simple concatenation
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Colorectal polyps are abnormal tissue growths that may develop into
malignant lesions if they are not detected and removed at an early
stage. Colonoscopy is routinely employed for examination of the lower
gastrointestinal tract and enables clinicians to identify and resect
suspicious polyps. However, the reliability of visual inspection is
affected by operator experience, fatigue, imaging conditions, and the
heterogeneous appearance of colorectal lesions. Computer-aided polyp
segmentation can assist clinical examination by providing pixel-level
localization of suspected lesions and by presenting their spatial extent
and boundaries more explicitly.

Accurate polyp segmentation remains challenging because colorectal
lesions exhibit substantial variations in size, morphology, texture,
color, and location. Some polyps occupy a large and visually distinctive
region, whereas others are small, flat, or partially concealed by
intestinal folds. In addition, visual similarity between lesion tissue
and the surrounding mucosa frequently produces weak or ambiguous
boundaries. Specular reflections, motion blur, bubbles, shadows, fecal
material, and medical instruments may further interfere with feature
extraction. These factors make it difficult for a segmentation model to
obtain reliable semantic localization and precise boundary delineation
simultaneously.

U-shaped convolutional architectures have been extensively used in
medical image segmentation because their contracting paths extract
hierarchical semantic features, while their expanding paths
progressively recover spatial resolution. Skip connections transfer
relatively high-resolution encoder representations to the decoder and
therefore facilitate lesion localization. Nevertheless, conventional
skip connections may also introduce redundant texture responses and
background interference because encoder features are transferred without
explicit feature selection. Moreover, repeated spatial down-sampling may
remove subtle structural information required for segmenting small or
weakly bounded polyps. U-Net established the fundamental
contracting-expanding design, while subsequent architectures, including
UNet++, redesigned skip pathways and incorporated deep supervision to
reduce the semantic discrepancy between encoder and decoder
representations {[}1,2{]}.

To improve global-context modeling, transformer-based and hybrid
convolution-transformer architectures have been investigated for medical
image segmentation. TransUNet combines convolutional feature extraction
with transformer encoding to capture long-range dependencies, while
Polyp-PVT employs hierarchical transformer representations and
cross-level feature aggregation for polyp segmentation {[}3,4{]}. These
methods demonstrate the value of global contextual information; however,
transformer-based designs can introduce considerable computational
complexity and may still require dedicated mechanisms for recovering
local boundary information.

Modern convolutional architectures provide an alternative means of
obtaining strong hierarchical representations without relying on
self-attention. ConvNeXt modernizes conventional convolutional design
through large-kernel depthwise convolution, inverted channel expansion,
Layer Normalization, GELU activation, layer scaling, and stochastic
depth. These characteristics enable ConvNeXt to retain the spatial
inductive biases and computational regularity of convolutional networks
while providing competitive representation capacity {[}5{]}.

Despite these advances, three interconnected limitations remain
important for polyp segmentation. First, the large variation in lesion
size requires contextual representations with multiple receptive fields.
Second, weak boundaries require a mechanism that selectively enhances
local structural variations rather than indiscriminately transferring
skip features. Third, deep semantic representations must be combined
with shallow spatial information without allowing noisy low-level
responses to dominate the final prediction. Addressing these limitations
within a computationally moderate architecture is particularly important
for applications in which both accuracy and practical inference
efficiency are required.

This study proposes a multi-scale and boundary-sensitive ConvNeXt-UNet
for automatic colorectal polyp segmentation. The model employs a
ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder to generate four levels of hierarchical
representations. A lightweight residual bottleneck and a Multi-Scale
Context module are applied to the deepest encoder feature to aggregate
local, enlarged-receptive-field, and global information. During
decoding, a Boundary-Sensitive Enhancement and Integration module
refines the concatenated encoder and decoder features by learning
spatial edge-response attention. In parallel, a shallow Detail Branch
preserves high-resolution appearance information directly from the input
image. A Gated Detail Fusion module selectively injects this detailed
representation into the final decoder stage. Three auxiliary output
heads provide intermediate supervision during training. The complete
architecture contains approximately 29.61 million trainable parameters.

The principal contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

1. A ConvNeXt-Tiny-based U-shaped segmentation architecture is developed
to combine hierarchical convolutional feature extraction with a
lightweight decoder for colorectal polyp segmentation.

2. A Multi-Scale Context module is introduced at the bottleneck to
aggregate features obtained using three dilated depthwise convolution
branches and a global-context branch.

3. A Boundary-Sensitive Enhancement and Integration module is designed
to refine skip-connected features through learned spatial edge-response
modulation.

4. A high-resolution Detail Branch and Gated Detail Fusion module are
introduced to preserve shallow spatial information and selectively
combine it with the final decoder representation.

5. Intermediate prediction heads are incorporated to support deep
supervision and improve optimization of multi-level decoder features.

6. The proposed model is evaluated on Kvasir-SEG, CVC-ClinicDB, CVC-300,
CVC-ColonDB, and ETIS-LaribPolypDB using in-distribution and
cross-dataset testing protocols.

\section{Related work}\label{related-work}

\subsection{Convolutional encoder-decoder
architectures}\label{convolutional-encoder-decoder-architectures}

Fully convolutional encoder-decoder networks form the foundation of
modern medical image segmentation. U-Net introduced a symmetric
contracting and expanding architecture in which high-resolution encoder
representations are transferred to the decoder through skip connections.
This design enables the network to combine contextual representations
with spatially detailed features and has become a standard baseline for
biomedical segmentation {[}1{]}.

Subsequent studies modified the U-shaped architecture to improve feature
fusion and optimization. UNet++ introduced nested dense skip pathways to
reduce the semantic discrepancy between encoder and decoder features and
employed deep supervision at multiple decoder depths {[}2{]}. Such
developments demonstrated that the design of skip connections is as
important as the choice of encoder because direct feature transfer may
combine representations with substantially different semantic
characteristics.

In polyp segmentation, computationally efficient convolutional networks
have also received considerable attention. HarDNet-MSEG combines a
low-memory-traffic convolutional backbone with a lightweight decoder and
illustrates that competitive segmentation performance can be obtained
without a computationally expensive transformer architecture {[}6{]}.
However, standard convolutional models generally require additional
mechanisms to capture broad contextual relationships and refine weak
lesion boundaries.

PraNet introduced a parallel partial decoder to aggregate high-level
features and a reverse-attention strategy to progressively refine
uncertain polyp regions {[}7{]}. Its recurrent interaction between
regional predictions and boundary cues demonstrated the effectiveness of
explicitly directing the decoder toward inadequately segmented regions.
Nevertheless, reverse-attention refinement depends on preceding
prediction maps and does not directly solve the problem of noisy
information transferred through conventional skip connections.

\subsection{Global and multi-scale contextual
modeling}\label{global-and-multi-scale-contextual-modeling}

The considerable variation in polyp size motivates the use of
multi-scale contextual modeling. A segmentation network must preserve
local details for small lesions while maintaining a sufficiently large
receptive field to identify extensive or irregularly shaped polyps.
Dilated convolutions, pyramid pooling, multi-branch receptive fields,
and cross-scale feature aggregation have consequently been incorporated
into many segmentation architectures.

Transformer-based approaches address this problem by modeling long-range
spatial relationships. TransUNet uses a transformer encoder over
convolutional feature representations to capture global context while
retaining high-resolution CNN features for localization {[}3{]}.
Polyp-PVT employs a pyramid vision transformer and specialized fusion
modules to integrate semantic and positional information across
hierarchical feature levels {[}4{]}. These methods improve long-range
feature interaction, but their attention operations and cross-level
fusion mechanisms may increase architectural and computational
complexity.

Recent contextual-information-flow and multi-stage architectures
similarly emphasize the need to coordinate local lesion appearance with
broader anatomical context. State-space and frequency-oriented
architectures have also been investigated. Polyp-Mamba combines
multi-frequency perception with a gated selection mechanism to model
spatial dependencies and frequency-sensitive representations {[}8{]}.
The present work does not employ frequency decomposition or a
state-space operator; instead, it uses parallel dilated depthwise
convolutions and global average pooling to obtain multi-scale contextual
information within a purely convolutional framework.

\subsection{Boundary-aware polyp
segmentation}\label{boundary-aware-polyp-segmentation}

Weak and visually ambiguous lesion boundaries are among the principal
difficulties in colorectal polyp segmentation. Boundary-aware networks
attempt to preserve or reconstruct structural information that may
disappear during repeated down-sampling. These methods commonly
introduce explicit edge supervision, auxiliary contour branches,
gradient-derived features, reverse attention, or boundary-sensitive
feature fusion.

MEGANet employs multi-scale edge-guided attention and incorporates
high-frequency information derived from image pyramids to improve
segmentation of weak boundaries {[}9{]}. Its findings demonstrate that
explicitly retaining edge-related information can reduce deterioration
of boundary features in deep networks. MSBP-Net performs multi-scale
boundary prediction to improve contour delineation while maintaining
relatively low model complexity {[}10{]}. This line of research
indicates that boundary information should be modeled at more than one
scale because polyp contours may contain both broad structural
transitions and small irregularities.

Explicit edge extraction may also respond to irrelevant structures such
as folds, reflections, bubbles, and instruments. Furthermore, additional
boundary labels or edge-generation procedures may increase the
complexity of the training pipeline. The BSEI module in the proposed
model follows a different strategy. It derives a learned spatial
response from transformed skip features and uses this response to
modulate the features through residual enhancement. Therefore, the
method is boundary-sensitive but does not depend on Sobel filtering,
Laplacian pyramids, explicit contour labels, or a separate boundary
loss.

\subsection{Detail preservation and adaptive feature
fusion}\label{detail-preservation-and-adaptive-feature-fusion}

Deep semantic features provide strong category discrimination but have
reduced spatial resolution. Conversely, shallow features preserve local
appearance and edge information but may contain substantial background
noise. Effective segmentation therefore requires controlled interaction
between semantic and detailed representations.

Conventional U-shaped networks concatenate or add shallow and deep
features directly. Although these operations are computationally simple,
they assign no explicit relevance to detailed information. Attention and
gating mechanisms provide a more selective alternative by estimating the
contribution of one representation conditioned on another. Recent
polyp-segmentation architectures employ specialized cross-scale or
attention-based fusion to suppress irrelevant responses and improve
lesion localization.

The proposed method addresses detail preservation using a shallow Detail
Branch operating at one-half of the input resolution. Instead of
directly adding this feature to the decoder, the Gated Detail Fusion
module estimates a gate from the concatenated decoder and detail
representations. The gate modulates the detail feature before final
fusion, enabling the network to retain high-resolution information while
limiting transfer of irrelevant low-level responses.

\subsection{Positioning of the proposed
method}\label{positioning-of-the-proposed-method}

Existing approaches have improved polyp segmentation through reverse
attention, transformer-based contextual modeling, frequency processing,
explicit boundary prediction, or complex multi-stage refinement.
Nevertheless, many methods address context, boundaries, and fine details
as separate problems. The proposed architecture integrates these
requirements within a unified convolutional encoder-decoder framework.

Unlike transformer-based methods, the model employs a ConvNeXt-Tiny
encoder and depthwise separable convolutions. Unlike frequency-oriented
networks, it does not require Fourier or wavelet decomposition. Unlike
explicitly supervised boundary networks, it derives a learned
local-response map from intermediate features. Finally, unlike direct
shallow-deep fusion, it uses a conditioned gate to control the
contribution of the high-resolution detail representation. These design
choices provide a coherent framework for investigating whether
multi-scale context, boundary-sensitive skip refinement, and selective
detail fusion can jointly improve polyp segmentation and cross-dataset
generalization.

\section{Method}\label{method}

\subsection{Overall architecture}\label{overall-architecture}

Let \(I \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}\) denote an input colonoscopy image. The proposed
network follows a U-shaped encoder-decoder configuration and consists of
five principal components: a ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder, a lightweight
residual bottleneck, a Multi-Scale Context module, a boundary-sensitive
decoder with refined skip connections, and a high-resolution Detail
Branch with gated fusion.

The encoder produces four hierarchical feature maps \{F1, F2, F3, F4\}
with channel dimensions \{96, 192, 384, 768\} and spatial resolutions
\{H/4×W/4, H/8×W/8, H/16×W/16, H/32×W/32\}. The deepest feature is
processed by the lightweight bottleneck and the Multi-Scale Context
module. The decoder then progressively increases spatial resolution. At
the first three decoding stages, the upsampled decoder feature is
concatenated with the corresponding encoder representation and processed
by BSEI. At one-half input resolution, the final decoder feature is
fused with the output of the Detail Branch. A refinement head converts
the fused representation into final segmentation logits, which are
bilinearly resized to the original image resolution. During training,
three auxiliary prediction heads produce additional full-resolution
logits from intermediate decoder stages.

The principal forward operations can be summarized as follows:

\emph{\{F1, F2, F3, F4\} = E(I),}

\emph{B = M\_MSC(M\_LB(F4)),}

\emph{D4 = M\_BSEI,4({[}U4(B), F3{]}),}

\emph{D3 = M\_BSEI,3({[}U3(D4), F2{]}),}

\emph{D2 = M\_BSEI,2({[}U2(D3), F1{]}),}

\emph{D1 = M\_BSEI,1(U1(D2)),}

\emph{P = U\_out(M\_ref(M\_GDF(D1, M\_detail(I)))).}

Here, {[}·,·{]} denotes channel-wise concatenation, Ui denotes the
corresponding decoder up-sampling operation, M\_LB is the lightweight
bottleneck, M\_MSC is the Multi-Scale Context module, M\_GDF is Gated
Detail Fusion, and P denotes the final segmentation logits.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{ ConvNeXt-Tiny
encoder}{ ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder}}\label{convnext-tiny-encoder}

Before entering the encoder, the input image is normalized using the
ImageNet channel mean and standard deviation:

\emph{\(\hat{I}_c = (I_c - \mu_c) / \sigma_c,\)}

\emph{\(\mu = (0.485, 0.456, 0.406), \sigma = (0.229, 0.224, 0.225).\)}

The first encoder stage uses a 4×4 convolution with stride 4, followed
by Layer Normalization. The subsequent three stages begin with Layer
Normalization and a 2×2 stride-2 convolution. The numbers of ConvNeXt
blocks in the four stages are 3, 3, 9, and 3, respectively.

For an input feature X, each ConvNeXt block applies a 7×7 depthwise
convolution, converts the representation to channels-last format for
Layer Normalization and linear channel projection, expands the channel
dimension by a factor of four, applies GELU activation, restores the
original channel dimension, and adds the transformed representation
through a layer-scaled stochastic residual connection.

\emph{Z = DWConv7×7(X),}

\emph{V = W2(GELU(W1(LN(Z)))),}

\emph{\(Y = X + \operatorname{DropPath}(\gamma \odot V).\)}

Spatial dropout is applied to the outputs of the second, third, and
fourth encoder stages. This regularization reduces excessive dependence
on individual feature channels in deeper semantic representations.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{ Lightweight residual
bottleneck}{ Lightweight residual bottleneck}}\label{lightweight-residual-bottleneck}

The deepest encoder representation F4 is first processed by a
lightweight residual bottleneck. A 1×1 convolution reduces its channel
dimension from 768 to 192. A depthwise separable 3×3 convolution
performs spatial transformation in the reduced feature space, after
which another 1×1 convolution restores the channel dimension to 768.

\emph{\(B_0 = F_4 + \gamma_b H(F_4).\)}

The transformation H(·) includes pointwise channel reduction, Layer
Normalization, GELU activation, depthwise separable convolution,
dropout, pointwise channel restoration, and final Layer Normalization.
The learnable scalar \(\gamma_b\) is initialized to zero, so the bottleneck
initially behaves as an identity mapping and progressively introduces
its residual transformation during optimization.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{ Multi-Scale Context
module}{ Multi-Scale Context module}}\label{multi-scale-context-module}

The Multi-Scale Context module is applied to B0 to enrich the bottleneck
representation using multiple receptive fields while maintaining
moderate computational complexity. A 1×1 convolution first reduces the
768-channel input to 96 channels.

\emph{R = GELU(LN(Conv1×1(B0))).}

Three parallel depthwise 3×3 convolutions with dilation rates \(d \in \{1,
3, 5\}\) are applied:

\emph{\(C_d = \operatorname{GELU}(\operatorname{LN}(\operatorname{DWConv}_{3\times3,d}(R))),\ d \in \{1, 3, 5\}.\)}

A fourth branch extracts image-level context through global average
pooling:

\emph{Cg = Up(GELU(Conv1×1(GAP(R)))).}

The four branches are concatenated and projected back to 768 channels:

\emph{C = {[}C1, C3, C5, Cg{]},}

\emph{\(B = B_0 + \gamma_c P(C).\)}

The projection P(·) consists of a 1×1 convolution, Layer Normalization,
GELU activation, and dropout. The scalar \(\gamma_c\) is initialized to zero.
Dilation rates 1, 3, and 5 provide local and progressively broader
neighborhoods, while the pooled branch supplies image-level context.

\subsection{Boundary-Sensitive Enhancement and Integration
module}\label{boundary-sensitive-enhancement-and-integration-module}

At each skip-connected decoder stage, the upsampled decoder feature is
concatenated with the corresponding encoder feature. The resulting
representation is refined using BSEI. Let Sl = {[}Ul, Fl{]} denote the
concatenated decoder and encoder features at level l.

\emph{Ql = GELU(LN(Conv1×1(Sl))),}

\emph{Zl = GELU(LN(SepConv3×3(Ql))).}

A learned depthwise 3×3 convolution generates a local response map. The
absolute value represents the magnitude of the learned spatial
variation, and a sigmoid bounds the response:

\emph{Al = sigmoid(\textbar DWConv3×3(Zl)\textbar).}

The enhanced feature is calculated by residual modulation:

\emph{\(Y_l = Z_l + A_l \odot Z_l.\)}

Locations with stronger learned spatial responses therefore receive
greater amplification. This operation should be described as learned
boundary-sensitive or edge-response enhancement, rather than explicit
boundary detection. It does not use Sobel filters, manually generated
edge maps, channel squeeze-and-excitation, or boundary annotations.

\subsection{Progressive decoder}\label{progressive-decoder}

Each decoder block consists of bilinear up-sampling by a factor of two,
a depthwise separable 3×3 convolution, Layer Normalization, GELU
activation, and spatial dropout. The first decoder stage transforms the
context-enhanced bottleneck from 768 to 384 channels. After
concatenation with F3, BSEI produces a 384-channel representation.

\emph{D4 = M\_BSEI,4({[}M\_dec,4(B), F3{]}),}

\emph{D3 = M\_BSEI,3({[}M\_dec,3(D4), F2{]}),}

\emph{D2 = M\_BSEI,2({[}M\_dec,2(D3), F1{]}),}

\emph{D1 = M\_BSEI,1(M\_dec,1(D2)).}

The output channel dimensions of D3 and D2 are 192 and 96, respectively.
The final decoder block increases the spatial resolution from H/4×W/4 to
H/2×W/2. No encoder skip feature is concatenated at this final stage
because the encoder stem begins at one-quarter input resolution.

\subsection{High-resolution Detail
Branch}\label{high-resolution-detail-branch}

The Detail Branch processes the original input independently of the deep
encoder. Its objective is to retain relatively high-resolution
appearance information that may be attenuated by the stride-4 encoder
stem. The branch begins with a 3×3 convolution with stride 2, producing
a 32-channel feature at one-half input resolution.

\emph{T0 = GELU(BN(Conv3×3,s=2(I))),}

\emph{T = GELU(BN(SepConv3×3(T0))).}

Dropout is applied after both operations. Unlike the main encoder and
decoder, this branch uses Batch Normalization.

\subsection{Gated Detail Fusion}\label{gated-detail-fusion}

The final decoder feature D1 and the detail representation T have the
same spatial resolution. Before fusion, the detail feature is projected
using a depthwise separable convolution:

\emph{\(\hat{T} = \operatorname{GELU}(\operatorname{BN}(\operatorname{SepConv}_{3\times3}(T))).\)}

A spatially varying gate is estimated from the concatenated decoder and
detail representations:

\emph{\(G = \operatorname{sigmoid}(W_{g2}(\operatorname{GELU}(W_{g1}([D_1, \hat{T}])))).\)}

The gate contains 32 channels and modulates the detail representation:

\emph{\(T_g = G \odot \hat{T}.\)}

The gated detail representation is concatenated with the decoder feature
and projected to 128 channels:

\emph{Ffuse = GELU(LN(Conv1×1({[}D1, Tg{]}))).}

The gate controls the spatial and channel-wise contribution of the
shallow detail representation before it enters the final prediction
head.

\subsection{Prediction head and deep
supervision}\label{prediction-head-and-deep-supervision}

The fused feature is processed by two depthwise separable convolutions.
The first reduces the channel dimension from 128 to 96, and the second
reduces it from 96 to 48. A 1×1 convolution then produces the
segmentation logits.

\emph{P0 = Conv1×1(R(Ffuse)),}

\emph{P = UpH,W(P0).}

For binary segmentation, the probability map at inference is obtained as
\(\hat{Y} = \operatorname{sigmoid}(P)\). The implementation returns logits rather than sigmoid
probabilities; consequently, the activation should be applied by the
loss function or by the inference procedure.

During training, auxiliary 1×1 prediction heads are attached to D2, D3,
and D4. Their logits are resized to the original input resolution:

\emph{P2 = UpH,W(Conv1×1(D2)),}

\emph{P3 = UpH,W(Conv1×1(D3)),}

\emph{P4 = UpH,W(Conv1×1(D4)).}

A general deeply supervised objective can be written as:

\emph{Ltotal = Lseg(P,Y) + $\lambda$2 Lseg(P2,Y) + $\lambda$3 Lseg(P3,Y) + $\lambda$4
Lseg(P4,Y).}

\emph{Here, Y denotes the reference mask and $\lambda$2, $\lambda$3, and $\lambda$4 control the
auxiliary losses. The exact segmentation loss and numerical auxiliary
weights are not defined in the supplied architecture file and must be
obtained from the training implementation before the manuscript is
finalized.}

\subsection{Model complexity}\label{model-complexity}

For a single-class output configuration, the supplied implementation
contains 29,609,302 trainable parameters (approximately 29.61 million).
This value is consistent with the approximately 29.60 million parameters
reported in the later complexity table of the experimental draft. Any

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Evaluation metrics
}{Evaluation metrics }}\label{evaluation-metrics}

To objectively evaluate model performance, two commonly used
segmentation metrics are employed: the mean Intersection over Union
(mIoU) and mean Dice coefficient (mDice). In addition, in order to
evaluate the performance of the model more comprehensively, this paper
also adds four evaluation indicators commonly used in the field of
salient object detection, namely S-measure (S$\alpha$) {[}ho-f20{]},
F-measure{[}{]}, E-measure () {[}ho-fan{]} and mean absolute error (MAE)
{[}ho-peraz{]}. The detailed definitions and formulas for these metrics
are as follows: mIoU and mDice are commonly used to measure the
similarity between the prediction map and the real map. They
quantitatively analyze the spatial overlap between the prediction map
and the real map. The higher the value, the better the segmentation
accuracy of the model. Among them, the average means that the IoU
coefficient and Dice co efficient of each category are averaged, but
there is only one category of polyps here. Thus, their calculation
formulas are as follows:

\[mIoU = \ \frac{TP}{TP + FN + FP}\]

\[mDice = \ \frac{2*TP}{2*TP + FP + FN}\]

Where TP (True Positive) indicates the real sample, that is, the
forecast is a positive sample, and the actual sample is also a positive
sample; FP (False Positive) indicates the false positive sample, that
is, the prediction is a positive sample, and the actual sample is a
negative sample; FN (False Negative) indicates the false negative
sample, that is, the prediction is a negative sample, and the actual
sample is a positive sample.

S-measure, \(S_{\alpha}\), is used to measure the structural similarity
between region perception (\(S_{r}\)) and object perception (\(S_{o}\))
between the predicted graph and the real graph. It comprehensively
considers the similarity of spatial structure and regional contour, and
is a comprehensive evaluation of segmentation performance. Thus, this
calculation formulas are as follows:

\[S_{\alpha} = \ \alpha*\ S_{\alpha} + (1 - \alpha)*\ S_{r}\]

Where $\alpha$ is the weight parameter, and the default setting is 0.5.

F-measure, \(F_{\beta}\), comprehensively considers the importance of
precision and recall rate, and gives corresponding weights to precision
and recall rate according to specific standards. It provides a balanced
evaluation index for the evaluation model. Thus, \(F_{\beta}\)
calculation formula is as follows:

\[F_{\beta} = \left( 1 + \beta^{2} \right)\frac{P*R}{\beta^{2}P + R}\]

Where P and Rare precision and recall respectively; \(\beta^{2}\)
parameter, which is set to 0.3.

E-measure,\(E_{\varphi}\) , is used to quantified alignment between
predicted and ground truth masks at image and pixel levels. Its
calculation formula is as follows:

\[E_{\varphi} = \ \frac{1}{W*H}\sum_{i = 1}^{W}{\sum_{j = 1}^{H}{\phi_{FM}(i,j),\ \ }}\]

Where \(\phi_{FM}\) is the enhanced alignment matrix {[}ho-40{]}.

MAE is used to compute the mean absolute error between predicted and
ground truth masks. The calculation formula is as follows:

\[MAE = \ \frac{1}{W*H}\sum_{i = 1}^{W}{\sum_{j = 1}^{H}{\left| T(i,j) - G(i,j) \right|,\ \ }}\]

Where T and G denote the predicted and ground truth masks, respectively.

\subsection{Implementation details}\label{implementation-details}

In this work, the proposed ConvNeXt-UNet was trained on a GeForce RTX
3090 GPU with 24 GB memory and a pytorch framework. the AdamW optimizer
was employed with a learning rate of 0.0001 and weight decay of 0.0004,
and the batch size was set to 16. All samples were resized to 352 ×352
before training. An early stopping mechanism was used to supervise the
training. Experiments indicated that the model converged to its optimal
state at approximately the 120th epoch.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{ablation-studies}

To systematically evaluate the individual contributions of the proposed
modules to the overall segmentation performance, a comprehensive
ablation study was conducted. We define our baseline model as the
ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder paired with a lightweight decoder and the final
prediction head, completely omitting the Multi-Scale Context (MSC)
module, BSEI, the Detail Branch (DB), Gated Detail Fusion (GDF), and
Deep Supervision (DS). By incrementally adding or systematically
removing these components, we isolated their specific impacts on the
network. The quantitative results of these architectural modifications
are summarized in Table XX.

\subsubsection{Effectiveness of MSC}\label{effectiveness-of-msc}

The Multi-Scale Context (MSC) module is designed to capture and
aggregate multi-scale contextual information, which is critical for
handling significant variations in target object sizes. To assess its
impact, we compared the baseline model with a configuration integrating
the MSC module. As shown in Table XX, the addition of the MSC module
effectively enhanced the network\textquotesingle s spatial adaptability,
yielding an improvement of XX\% in mDice and XX\% in mIoU on the XX
dataset. This confirms the module\textquotesingle s capability to enrich
feature representations prior to the decoding stage.

enhance boundary spatial features / integrate edge-level information{]}
during the feature extraction process. In our ablation experiments,
removing the BSEI module from the combined network led to a noticeable
degradation in segmentation accuracy, particularly in {[}describe
specific area, e.g., complex boundary regions{]}. Specifically, the

XX). This demonstrates that the BSEI module successfully contributes to

\subsubsection{Effectiveness of DB}\label{effectiveness-of-db}

The Detail Branch (DB) was specifically developed to preserve
high-resolution spatial details that are typically lost as features pass
through the deeper, heavily down-sampled stages of the encoder.
Discarding the DB in the ablation experiments resulted in overly

The Gated Detail Fusion (GDF) module acts as a selective mechanism to
optimally combine the high-resolution spatial features from the DB with

\subsubsection{Effectiveness of DS}\label{effectiveness-of-ds}

Finally, Deep Supervision (DS) is employed during the training phase to
